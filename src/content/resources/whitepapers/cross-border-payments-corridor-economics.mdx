## Executive summary

Cross-border payments remain expensive and slow relative to domestic rails, despite a decade of fintech innovation. This paper examines why — the FX spread, the correspondent banking chain, the compliance overhead, and the liquidity model — and describes how IDMB's corridor infrastructure approaches each problem.

Key conclusions:
- The cost of a cross-border payment is dominated by FX spread and correspondent banking fees, not technology costs
- Pre-funded corridors with local disbursement networks outperform correspondent banking chains on cost and speed for corridors where volume justifies the float requirement
- Compliance in cross-border is harder than in domestic payments because it involves two or more regulatory regimes simultaneously

## Part 1: Why cross-border payments are expensive

### The correspondent banking chain

A traditional cross-border wire travels through a chain of correspondent banks: the sending bank, one or more intermediary banks with bilateral agreements, and the receiving bank. Each bank in the chain:
- Charges a fee (typically $10–$35 per transaction, charged to the sender, receiver, or shared)
- Adds processing time (each institution has its own batch window or availability window)
- Applies compliance checks that may require manual review

For a payment from a UK bank to a Kenyan bank, the chain typically involves 3–4 institutions and takes 2–5 business days.

### The FX spread

The exchange rate a retail customer sees includes:
- The mid-market FX rate (the rate you see on Google)
- A spread charged by each bank in the chain
- A retail markup charged by the sending institution's FX desk

Combined, these mark-ups typically add 2–4% to the cost of the transaction on top of the fixed fees. On a $200 remittance — a common transfer size in the remittance corridor — 3% plus $25 in fees represents 15–16% of the transaction value.
