## The rip-and-replace fallacy

Between 2015 and 2022, at least a dozen mid-sized mobile money operators attempted a full platform replacement. The pattern was consistent: a new core vendor, a big bang cutover, a migration project that ran 18 months over budget, and in most cases, a partial rollback to the old system for at least two years during the "parallel run."

The customers never noticed the new platform. They noticed the outages.

The lesson that took too long to learn: **mobile money modernisation is not a core banking replacement problem. It's a data migration problem, a float reconciliation problem, and an agent trust problem.** The technology is the easiest part.

## What users actually care about

Mobile money users care about three things in order: availability, float accuracy, and speed. Most of them interact with the system through a USSD menu on a $40 handset with intermittent network coverage. They do not know or care what runs the ledger.

What this means for a modernisation: the USSD menu, the agent app, the float management logic and the SMS confirmation messages are the user-facing product. The ledger is the engine room. You can refit the engine room without touching the bridge.

## The overlay approach

The architecture that works: run the new core in parallel, migrate product lines one at a time, and use the existing system as the system of record until each product line is fully migrated.

1. **Start with new customer accounts**: every new account opened goes onto the new ledger. Existing accounts stay on legacy.
2. **Migrate dormant accounts first**: accounts with zero balance and no transactions in 90 days carry zero reconciliation risk. Move them in bulk.
3. **Migrate by agent cluster**: mobile money agent networks are geographically clustered. Migrating one region at a time bounds the blast radius of any reconciliation issue.
4. **Keep the USSD bridge up throughout**: the old USSD menu routes to a translation layer that can talk to either ledger. Customers never see a change.

This approach typically takes 12–18 months for a 1–3 million account book. That's longer than the vendor slide deck says, but shorter than a failed big bang.

## Float is the hardest part

The agent float model in mobile money is the piece that no core banking vendor understands until they try to migrate it. Agents hold prefunded float — essentially a credit balance — which they use to cash in and cash out for customers. The float reconciliation happens at end of day (or sometimes continuously with newer systems).

Migrating float to a new ledger requires:
- An exact, audited snapshot of every agent's float balance at cutover
- A dual-write period where float movements are recorded on both systems
- A reconciliation report that the agent network operations team can actually read

The IDMB float management module was built specifically for this. It models agent float as a first-class wallet type, supports dual-write during migration, and generates the end-of-day float report in the format that agent operations teams are used to seeing.

## Regulatory coordination

Mobile money licences in most markets (Central Bank of Kenya, Bank of Ghana, BCEAO, RBI) require regulatory notification for a core platform change. In some markets, it requires approval. Starting that conversation 6 months before the first account migration is not optional.

The IDMB regulatory team has completed this process in 14 markets. We have template notification letters, a standard data room, and relationships with the relevant regulatory contacts. This is not advertised prominently in the product documentation, but it's one of the most valuable things we offer.

## The short version

Mobile money modernisation succeeds when you treat it as a migration problem, not a replacement. Keep what works (brand, agents, USSD, float model), change what's limiting you (ledger throughput, API surface, compliance tooling). The IDMB platform is designed for exactly this overlay architecture.
